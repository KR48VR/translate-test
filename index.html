One school, one January

When AI joined the band

Mr Zhu Ruixiong (朱锐雄) has run a percussion music school in Singapore for more than ten years. In January this year, the school brought in AI tools — and its everyday work changed almost beyond recognition.

AI is reshaping how most of us work and where our careers can go — and it is changing what skills employers pay for. The wave started in tech, but it has long since spread to every field.

Education, Mr Zhu's industry, is already feeling its force — and it is far from the only one being disrupted.

So what is AI actually doing to Singapore's job market? What does that mean for ordinary working people? And how can we get ready for what comes next?

Today's trends

Which industries in Singapore want AI skills most?

Lianhe Zaobao worked with the employment platform Jobstreet by SEEK to measure how often AI-related keywords have appeared in Singapore job advertisements over the past three years. The more often AI turns up in an industry's job ads, the harder that industry is hunting for AI skills.

Jobstreet did not disclose exact advertisement counts, but its website's search function shows close to 60,000 full-time and part-time positions currently listed.

Since AI went mainstream in 2023, these five industries have topped the table every single year. AI keywords appear far more often in their ads than anywhere else — on average about 60% more often than in sixth-placed construction & engineering.

Computing & IT (software) sits at the very front of the AI era and has ranked first for three straight years. The jobs themselves are changing: beyond traditional coding, employers now ask for AI orchestration, security and governance, and systems integration.

Education depends on human contact, so it is usually seen as one of the safer industries in the AI era. Yet over the past three years its demand for AI skills has climbed sharply — from fourth place in 2023 to second place last year. One likely reason is the sector's chronic manpower shortage: new AI-enabled roles can streamline lesson planning, teaching materials and back-office admin. Mr Zhu's music school is exactly this story.

HR & consulting is a special case. Because the industry prizes interpersonal skills, employers hiring for AI roles there are less fixated on deep technical expertise. That reflects a wider trend: the rise of the 'new-collar' worker — someone who combines technology with real-world experience, adapts quickly and solves problems.

Which AI skills are employers asking for?

The most common AI keyword in Jobstreet's ads is broad analytical ability — analysis that goes beyond data alone. Other frequent requirements include data analysis, Python programming, computer science and automation.

The cooling-off is over: every industry is back on the AI trail

The top five stand at the front line of the AI shift, and their demand for AI talent is growing strongly. The remaining industries, after a brief hesitation during AI's messy early phase, are now drawing up AI talent strategies of their own.

−74%The large language model ChatGPT burst onto the scene at the end of 2022 and set off an AI hiring boom. Then Singapore employers hit pause: between 2024 and 2025, AI job ads plunged 74%. The technology's chaotic early growth had created real uncertainty, and companies entered a 'cooling-off period' — re-evaluating their AI investments and hiring far more cautiously.
×3Over the past year the picture flipped. As practical uses of AI became clearer, employers restarted their AI talent strategies, and the number of AI job ads roughly tripled year on year. Lee's explanation: two years of maturing technology have taught companies how to fold AI into their workflows, and hiring demand has followed.
−17%Job seekers have not kept pace. In the year to April 2026, applications for AI roles fell another 17%. The drop is smaller than the previous year's 39%, but it signals that local AI talent remains in short supply — many candidates simply do not yet have the skills employers urgently want.

Zhang's bigger point: AI is pushing Singapore's workplace from a competition of knowledge towards a competition of judgment. The most competitive people in future will not be those who hold the most information, but those who best combine AI's capabilities with human wisdom to create value together.

What's coming

What could the job market look like next?

Across most industries, AI's full impact has not yet arrived. That is not the same as saying your job will be spared.

AI is widely seen as the engine of the fourth industrial revolution, and major international studies have already sketched how far the wave could reach.

The full impact may take 10 years to show

Research by the investment bank Goldman Sachs on the US job market finds that AI currently weighs heavily on only a handful of industries.

It estimates that most fields will need about ten years to reach large-scale AI adoption. During that long transition, 6% to 7% of US workers could lose their jobs.

Worldwide, an estimated 300 million positions are exposed to AI automation.

By 2030, the global job mix will be different

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 projects that by 2030, driven by AI, the green transition and economic turbulence, 22% of jobs worldwide will go through structural change.

By then, some 92 million roles are expected to be displaced — but around 170 million new ones created. That is a net gain of 78 million jobs.

Source: World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report 2025; ILOSTAT

How to prepare

How do you prepare for the post-AI era?

AI brings far more opportunity than threat. The real challenge, for individuals and companies alike, is managing the transition — steering yourself towards the new jobs the post-AI era creates.

Research from the University of Oxford suggests AI skills already pay off — in wages, benefits and interview chances:

In a fast-moving AI era, which skills actually last?

What really matters is not just learning to operate AI, but knowing how to use it to raise the value of your work — while building the judgment, communication and problem-solving skills that AI cannot easily replace.

Prof Steven Wong (黄开庄), Director of the Centre for Digital Enablement at the Singapore Institute of Technology, cautions that AI is moving so fast that some of today's AI skills will become obsolete — especially operating tricks tied to specific tools.

Even so, his advice is to build AI literacy early, whatever direction the technology takes: learn to use AI, to supervise it, and to combine it with human judgment.

As AI spreads, anyone can use it to work faster. Simply 'knowing how to use AI' will not stay an advantage for long.

Poon King Wang (潘竞宏), Chief Strategy and Design-AI Officer at the Singapore University of Technology and Design and Director of the Lee Kuan Yew Centre for Innovative Cities, urges workers to shift their mindset from 'AI user' to 'AI creator' — using AI to keep creating value that is distinctly their own. That, he argues, is how you stand out in the future workplace.

Keep building transferable skills

Analytical thinking, communication, creativity and deep domain knowledge travel with you from job to job. Use AI deliberately to streamline your workflow: hand the repetitive tasks to the machine, and spend your own time on judgment, decisions and problem-solving.

At Singapore Management University, Prof Li Jia (李嘉), Dean of the School of Economics, and his team are tracking AI's long-term effect on the local job market.

He says it is impossible to predict precisely how many Singapore jobs will disappear to AI in the next three to five years. What researchers can do is watch hiring ads, spot industries where recruitment is shrinking, and sound the alarm early — giving policymakers and job seekers time to respond.

Much remains uncertain, including how far large language models can still advance — and that uncertainty will shape the job market's future. For individuals, Li's advice is to 'train your body' before the storm arrives.

He compares learning AI skills to taking medicine after a health check finds a problem, or exercising to build strength: the results are not guaranteed and rarely instant, but they never make you worse off. When the AI shock reaches your industry, those skills could be what keeps you employed — and what makes you more resilient.
